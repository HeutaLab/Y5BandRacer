1.2Whole team

The Rules

Every Formula 1 car on the grid is different. Every one of them was built to the same rulebook. Find out why that rulebook exists, and what it does to a car that breaks it.

Your sources

One rulebook, ten different cars

Formula 1 is run to a set of technical regulations written by the FIA, the organisation in charge of the sport. The regulations say how long a car may be, how heavy it must be, what shape the floor can be, and hundreds of other things.

Teams do not share designs. Each one reads the same rulebook and builds its own car, which is why the cars look similar but never identical. Without a rulebook, the team with the most money would build the biggest engine and win every race, and nobody would watch.

Engineers live by an unwritten rule: anything the rulebook does not forbid is allowed. Teams read the rules hunting for gaps, and some of the cleverest cars in history came from finding one.

Scrutineering: the check before the race

Before a car races, officials called scrutineers measure it, weigh it and inspect it. If it fails, it can be excluded, and the driver loses everything from that race even if they crossed the line first. Cars have been thrown out for being under the weight limit by less than a kilogram.

Your car faces the same check in Lesson 9. It must pass a gauge box of 160 × 80 × 65 mm standing on its own four wheels, without anybody forcing it 2.2.

Rules are written by people, so people change them

For 2026 Formula 1 rewrote a large part of its rulebook. Cars became lighter and shorter, the floor narrower, and about half the car's power now comes from electricity.

The biggest change: 2026 cars have wings that move while the car is driving, lying flat on the straights for speed and tilting up in the corners for grip. Moveable wings were banned for decades. Your 6.5 still bans them. Formula 1 changed its mind.

1.3BodyworkResearch strand

Air and the Body

You own the nose cone and the main body. Both of them are shapes that have to push air out of the way. Find out what the air is actually doing, then work out whether it is doing it to a car the size of your hand.

Your sources

Drag: the air pushing back

Air is thin, but it is not nothing. Anything moving through it has to shove it aside, and the air shoves back. That backwards push is drag.

Here is the part that matters. Drag does not simply double when you double your speed. It goes up four times. Three times faster and it is nine times bigger. At 300 km/h a Formula 1 car is pushing against something close to a wall of air, and most of its power goes on that alone.

Frontal area: the size of the hole you punch

The other half of drag is frontal area — how big a hole the car makes in the air, seen from directly in front. A taller, wider car punches a bigger hole and drags more.

This is why Formula 1 cars are so low, and why the driver lies almost flat. It is also why your main body has to stay low, while the rear wing is allowed to stand tall: the wing is thin, so it costs little frontal area for the job it does.

Two speeds, side by side

Aerodynamics is not one answer for every vehicle. It depends enormously on how fast the thing is going.

1.4AerodynamicistBodyworkResearch strand

Wings and Downforce

You own the rear wing. Before you draw one, find out what a wing is actually for, and whether a car that only goes in a straight line needs one at all.

Your sources

A wing fitted upside down

An aeroplane wing is shaped so the air pushes it up. Turn it upside down and the air pushes down instead. That push is downforce, and it presses the tyres into the track so they grip harder.

A Formula 1 car at full speed makes more downforce than the car weighs. People like to say it could drive along a tunnel ceiling. That is roughly true, and it is entirely because of wings and the shape of the floor.

Downforce is never free

A wing big enough to make downforce also makes drag. Every team in Formula 1 spends the season arguing about how much wing to fit, because more wing means faster corners and slower straights. At a track with one enormous straight they take wing off. At a twisty track they bolt it back on.

There is a second cost, and it is the one that matters to you. Pressing the tyres down harder makes them grip more, but it also makes them harder to roll. A car pressed into the ground rolls less far.

Your track is a straight line

1.5Running GearResearch strand

Wheels and Grip

You own the wheels, the tyres and the band. Everything the engine does has to get to the ground through your parts, and most of it does not make it.

Your sources

Why a racing car has rubber tyres

A Formula 1 tyre is soft rubber, deliberately. Soft rubber grips, and grip is what lets the car put its power into the road instead of spinning uselessly. The cost is that soft tyres wear out, sometimes in twenty laps, which is why races have pit stops at all.

Your tyres are rubber bands sitting in a groove in your wheel. They must stand proud of the rim 5.3, so the rubber touches the floor and the plastic does not. A groove cut half a millimetre too deep means the band never reaches the ground and your car spins on bare plastic.

Wheelspin: the power that goes nowhere

When a driver accelerates too hard the tyres break traction and spin, and the car goes nowhere while the wheels go everywhere. Formula 1 cars have careful electronic launch systems to stop exactly this at the start of a race.

Your car has no electronics. It has a stretched band that dumps all its energy in the first second, straight into two driven wheels. Wheelspin at launch is the single biggest thing that will steal your distance, and grip is your only defence.

Big wheels, small wheels

A big wheel travels further for each turn of the axle, so the same wound band carries the car further. But a big wheel is heavier, it is harder to get turning in the first place, and it lifts the car higher into the air.

A small wheel spins up easily and keeps the car low, but each turn of the axle buys you less ground. This is a genuine trade and Formula 1 argues about it too: in 2026 the cars moved to narrower tyres, giving up some grip to gain less drag and less weight.

1.6Optional · between the lessons

Livery Homework

Optional, and not assessed. If you want to think about what your car will look like before we design what it is, this is where to put the ideas.

Worth knowing first

What a livery is

A team's livery is its colours and markings. For most of racing history the colour told you the country: green for Britain, red for Italy, blue for France, silver for Germany. Fans recognise a team from a blur of colour long before they can read a word on it.

What a sponsor is buying

A large share of a racing team's money comes from sponsors who pay to put their name on the car. What they are buying is seconds of attention. A car crosses a television camera in a fraction of a second. If the logo cannot be recognised in that fraction, the money was wasted.

Your car is no longer than 160 mm

Designs that work small and fast all follow the same rules: few colours, big simple shapes, strong contrast. Designs that fail: thin outlines, which vanish; gradients, which turn to mush; small text, which becomes a grey smudge; and more than about four colours, which stops being a pattern and becomes noise.

2.1Whole team · 10 minutes

What You Are Given

Formula 1 teams do not design everything. Some parts are the same for every car on the grid, set by the rules, and the design work happens around them. Yours works the same way.

The base frame is not yours to change

Every team gets the same 3D printed chassis frame. It is 85 mm long, it carries both axles, and it has a hook underneath for the elastic band to latch onto. You do not design it, you do not cut it, and you cannot make it longer.

What you do design: the wheels, the tyre grooves, the nose cone, the main body with its band channel, and the rear wing. Five parts, three owners, one gauge box.

Two limits that are new, and why they exist

A minimum mass of 28 g new. You are allowed to cut holes in your parts to save print time, and you should. But a car made entirely of holes is a fragile car, and a light car spins its wheels at launch instead of moving. If your finished car comes in under 28 g you must add declared ballast 3.3 to make the weight up. Formula 1 has exactly this rule, for exactly the first of those reasons.

A maximum ground clearance of 25 mm new. The frame sits at half your wheel diameter, minus 4.5 mm. Big wheels lift it. There is a wheel size at which the car stops being a racing car and becomes a tractor, and the rulebook decides where that is. Work out for yourself what the biggest legal wheel therefore is, because it is not the 65 mm that Rule 2.7 seems to offer.

2.2BodyworkDesign bay

Nose Cone and Main Body

Two parts, both low, both sitting on a frame you cannot change. The body has one job you must not forget: the band has to get past it.

The band channel is not optional

The elastic band runs from the hook under the front of the frame back to the rear axle, where it winds up. Your main body sits on top of the frame, in the band's way. So the underside of your body needs a channel cut along its centre line for the band to run through.

Make it too narrow and the band rubs, which steals distance and eventually saws through the band. Make the channel open downwards and the printer can build it with no supports at all. Minimum 6 mm wide and 4 mm deep, and it runs the whole length of the body.

The Rear Wing

The one part of your car allowed to stand tall. Before you fill in a single number, settle the question you researched in Lesson 1: does this car get a wing at all?

Three rules bite on the wing at once

It may not hang too far back. 6.6 allows 20 mm behind the rear axle centreline. The axle is already 8.5 mm in from the back of the frame, so your wing may overhang the frame by 11 mm at most.

It may not hide a wheel. 6.3 is an open-wheel formula: all four wheels must be visible looking straight down. A wide wing casts a shadow over the rear wheels.

Every millimetre it hangs back is a millimetre of distance. 9.2 measures to the rearmost point of your stopped car. Your wing is probably the rearmost point.

2.4Running GearDesign bay

Wheels and Tyres

Everything the band does reaches the ground through your four wheels. Measure your own band first, because two of these numbers come out of that measurement and not out of this table.

Measure the band, then work out the groove

Take the callipers to the rubber band you have been issued. Measure its width and its thickness. Then:

Groove width = band width + 0.4  ·  Groove depth = band thickness − 0.5

The half millimetre is what makes the tyre stand proud of the rim 5.3. Cut the groove as deep as the band is thick and the rubber sits flush, the plastic touches the floor, and the car spins in place. Groove width must also leave 1.5 mm of rim on each side, so it can never be wider than your wheel width minus 3.

F2Running GearBuild in Tinkercad

Axles and Bushings

Your wheels have to turn, run straight and never rub on the car. Three parts make that happen: a hole, an axle and a bushing.

The hole is bigger than the axle

The axle hole is 6 mm and the axle is 5.5 mm. That leaves a gap of a quarter of a millimetre all the way round, about as thick as two sheets of paper, so the axle spins freely. Make them the same size and the plastic grips the axle tight.

A bushing stops the wheel bashing the body

A bushing is a short, thick tube round the axle hole that sticks out of the side of the car. The wheel can only touch the small end of the bushing, not the whole side of the body, so it spins freely and runs straight. Leave a 1 mm gap between the bushing and the wheel.

One wheel with its axle, one that pushes on

Each axle is joined to one wheel. Slide it through the car, then push the other wheel onto the end. That wheel's hole is 5.5 mm, the same as the axle, so it grips. The hole stops 1 mm before the outside, so the wheel stops in the right place.

Holes and bushings

In Tinkercad

  1. Axle height is half your wheel size. A 24 mm wheel puts the axle 12 mm up.
  2. Bushing: a Cylinder, Solid, 10 mm across, Sides 64. Turn it on its side so it runs across the car. Make it 4 mm longer than the body is wide, so 2 mm sticks out each side. Raise it to axle height − 5.
  3. Hole: a Cylinder, Hole, 6 mm across, Sides 64, on its side and longer than the bushing. Raise it to axle height − 3.
  4. Centre the hole in the bushing with Align L, and move both to where the axle goes.
  5. Select the body, the bushing and the hole, then Group. Do the same at the other axle.
  6. Look from the Front on the view cube. Both holes should line up with the centres of the wheels.

Why Sides 64? Tinkercad's cylinders start with 20 flat sides. 64 makes the hole and the axle properly round, so the axle spins smoothly.

Wheels and axles

In Tinkercad

  1. Fixed wheel: a Cylinder, Solid, 5.5 mm across, Sides 64, starting at the wheel's outside face. Centre it on the wheel with Align, then Group.
  2. Push-on wheel: a Cylinder, Hole, 5.5 mm across, Sides 64, from the inside face, stopping 1 mm before the outside face. Group.
  3. How long? The axle sticks out of the fixed wheel by the bushing length, plus 2 mm for the two gaps, plus the depth of the push-on hole.

Example: bushing 44 mm long, wheels 10 mm wide. The push-on hole is 9 mm deep, so the axle sticks out 44 + 2 + 9 = 55 mm. Across the wheels it is 44 + 2 + 10 + 10 = 66 mm, inside the 80 mm limit.

F3BodyworkBuild in Tinkercad

Ground Clearance and Tow Hook

The body sits 5 mm off the ground. The tow hook is the one part allowed lower: it hangs 3 mm below the body, so it ends 2 mm above the ground.

5 mm under the body

The wheels stand on the workplane. The underside of the body is 5 mm above it, all the way along. Nothing else on the car may be lower: not the nose, the wings or the bushings.

In Tinkercad

  1. Put the wheels on the workplane with Drop D.
  2. Select the body on its own, before you add the hook.
  3. Drag the black cone on top of it until the height reads 5.
  4. Look from the Front and the Right on the view cube. There should be a gap under the whole body.

Build the tow hook

In Tinkercad

  1. Post: a Box, Solid, 3 × 3 mm and 4 mm tall. Raise it 2 mm, so 1 mm goes up into the body and 3 mm hangs below.
  2. Foot: a Box, Solid, 3 mm wide, 3 mm long and 1 mm tall. Raise it 2 mm and push it against the back of the post.
  3. Move the hook under the nose, about 10 mm back from the front, and centre it on the body with Align L.
  4. Group the hook with the body. Its bottom is now 2 mm above the workplane, the lowest point on the car.

The foot points backwards, so a loop of string or elastic band cannot slip off the bottom when the car is pulled forwards. Keep the hook to exactly 3 mm. It is the only part that touches the printer plate, so the printer has to build supports under the rest of the car. On one of our test cars the hook added about 40 minutes to the print.
